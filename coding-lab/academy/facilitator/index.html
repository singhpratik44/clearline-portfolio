The build-and-play workshop

Give each child a turn as a designer and a turn as someone else's first player. By the end, each creator can show one decision they made and explain what happened when someone tried it.

30 minutes · Pairs

One shared device, two copies of a project, scrap paper and a pencil. One adult can guide a pair. In a larger group, give each pair the same role cards.

Run the first session

Set up two independent drafts

For a maze, open the Game Creator in two tabs. Label the tabs A and B on a slip of paper. Keep them open while partners swap. Before closing, download each draft separately. The on-device save slot is shared across tabs. For a story or simulation, use two sheets or two separate project files with the project studio.

Challenge. Make two routes to the same place. Change one tile, rule, or story choice so the player has a reason to pick a route.

  1. 0–2 min

    Offer a choice

    Each creator picks a feeling: surprise, suspense, calm, or their own. Ask, “What do you want your player to discover?” A coin can stand for anything the child chooses.

  2. 2–7 min

    A builds, B predicts

    A controls the first draft. Start A’s clock when the editor or materials are ready. A changes one feature on purpose and tries it. B points to where they think a player will go. Say, “Show me the part you chose.”

  3. 7–12 min

    B builds, A predicts

    Switch hands and drafts. Start a fresh five-minute clock for B. A listens while B picks a different goal or rule. If B gets stuck, offer one hint: “What could change if you move just one thing?”

  4. 12–16 min

    B plays A’s creation

    A says only the goal. B plays or traces their route. A watches the first choice without narrating or correcting it. Ask B, “What did you think would happen?”

  5. 16–20 min

    A plays B’s creation

    Swap again. B watches one moment, then records it with a mark, sketch, or short sentence. Use “My player tried…” An adult may write down the child's words.

  6. 20–25 min

    Choose what to change or keep

    Give both creators a turn. Each picks one edit or keeps a feature on purpose. Replay the affected moment. Ask, “What did you see that helped you decide?”

  7. 25–30 min

    Save and show

    Keep each version and one sentence, sketch, or dictated explanation in the learner’s portfolio. The child chooses whether to show a partner, a family member, or keep the project private. Record only anonymous counts in the pilot tally.

Invent a playable world

In this 30-minute session, two makers create one world together. Each contributes a rule or scene and watches someone try it. Open World Builder on one device per pair. Use three paper scene cards and counters if a learner prefers to direct without typing.

  1. 0–5 min

    Pick a question

    Choose something the player gets to decide. For a game, could moths carry enough light to open a moon gate? For a story, which memories would persuade a sleeping island to speak? For a simulation, how much rain would let a garden bloom? Invent your own world or adapt a starter. Name what the world counts.

  2. 5–13 min

    Connect three scenes

    Maker A drives while B sketches the start, a discovery and an ending. Add a start choice that leads to the discovery and changes the counter. Connect the discovery to the ending with a choice that needs a minimum amount. Each maker picks what one choice means to the player.

  3. 13–20 min

    Predict, trace and debug

    Swap the controls. Try a starting amount of 1 and a discovery choice that adds 1. B predicts whether an ending that needs 3 will open. A moves counters on paper while B plays. The total is 2, so the ending stays locked. Decide together whether to lower the requirement, add another way to earn a point, or change the starting amount. Replay from the start.

  4. 20–26 min

    Hand it to a first player

    Invite another pair or the facilitator to play without a walkthrough. Watch their first choice. Each maker points out a moment of surprise or confusion and chooses one change or explains why a feature should stay.

  5. 26–30 min

    Keep the world and its evidence

    Export the playable HTML. Save the editable project file for a later revision. Each maker finishes “At first we expected ___. When we played, ___. I chose ___ because ___.” A sketch, spoken explanation or paper trace works too.

Adjust the challenge

For a first build, let a partner type while the learner changes one choice in a starter and predicts its outcome. For a stretch, add a second path to the same ending. Find the smallest starting amount that still lets a player finish both paths.

Look for one design decision

For each maker you observed, mark whether they predicted a result, traced the counter and explained a decision after play. Record the number observed alongside each count. For a family playtest, let the maker choose whether to share the HTML. Ask the guest, “Which choice did you want to try twice, and why?”

Make both roles accessible

Builder and first player

Builder. “I want you to try ___. I’ll watch what you do.” The builder chooses the rule and decides which feedback to act on.

First player. “I expected ___. I tried ___. I noticed ___.” Describe one moment. Hand control back after the test.

Adult guide. Offer a choice between tracing the route and changing one object. Wait for the child's signal before touching the project.

Offer a different way in

  • Read prompts aloud or let a partner transcribe. A drawing, gesture, or oral explanation counts.
  • Use large paper squares, raised tape, or labeled shapes. Distinguish walls, tokens, and finish by shape or label.
  • A partner can operate the controls while the creator directs each change. Record whose design decision it was.
  • Allow quiet observation and private show-and-tell. Make the time goal optional. An untimed or paper session can be counted separately.
  • If a device is unavailable, use a token and paper grid. For a story use two choice cards. For a simulation use one changeable rule and two trial runs.

Host a family arcade

Set up a 15-minute showcase

  1. Before guests arrive. Ask each maker what they want to share. Use a project title on the station card. Keep private drafts off display.
  2. Minutes 0–3. Makers load their projects or set up a paper station. Pair every guest with a maker.
  3. Minutes 3–9. Run two three-minute rounds. Guests play for one minute, say what surprised them, and rotate. Makers can ask another person to present for them.
  4. Minutes 9–13. Makers show their first and latest versions and tell the story of one choice.
  5. Minutes 13–15. Let each maker decide what feedback to keep and whether to keep, export, or take down the display.

Put this card at each station

“Try my project. I’ll tell you the goal, then I’d like to see what you do.”

Maker. “At first I tried ___. After the playtest, I kept or changed ___ because ___.”

Guest. “I noticed ___ when I tried ___. What are you thinking of making next?”

For group feedback, invite one specific observation for every creator. Send project files home only when their creator chooses to share.

Show families what changed

Ask the maker to choose one project stage and demonstrate its skill. In the skills report, record what happened and who observed it. Keep the first attempt and the revision as separate entries. At pickup, invite the maker to explain one difference, then decide whether to print or save a copy.

Record a classroom pilot

Use one tally for one session and one medium. First-round target: each beginner has a chance to author and play an intentional change within five minutes of starting. Count each learner once per row. Start an empty tally for a new session.

Anonymous counts

Blank means unobserved. Enter 0 only when the count was actually zero. Counts stay in this browser until you clear them.

Read the evidence

  • Enter observations to calculate the three rates.

Record attendance and timed starts together so a quick result from one child is visible as one observation. Treat an unobserved or untimed learner as missing for the timed measure.

Next session. If creators wait on instructions, simplify the first choice. If partners take over, model silent observation and run the swap again. Change one thing and record another session using the same definitions.

These are classroom observations. A larger or different group may work differently. The download includes counts and denominators only.

Bell Tower Arcade

Someone has to ring the town bell before dark. Design the streets, invent a reason to take the long way, and make the final move feel earned.

What you’ll make A playable maze and a Scratch scene that teaches one of its rules.

Adjust the challenge

Start small. Build the opening with one coin and one finish. You can point to tiles while a partner places them.

Go further. Make two routes teach the same rule in different ways. Watch which one a new player chooses.

  1. 1
    Design

    Hide the bell key

    Give a coin a job. It might be a bell key, a missing gear, or something only your town needs. Place it where a new player will spot it.

    Show it working
    Let someone take the first three moves. Ask what they thought they were looking for.
    Keep as evidence
    Record their first move and one tile you kept or changed because of it.

  2. 2
    Rules and decisions

    Teach the tower rule

    Put the finish near the start and the coin down a side street. Let players visit the tower before they have its key.

    Show it working
    Walk to the finish with zero coins. Collect the key, return to the same square, and show what changes.
    Keep as evidence
    Draw both trips. Label the condition that made the second trip end differently.

    Sequencing

    Give the bell a voice

    In Scratch, draw a bell sprite. Make one keypress change its costume, pause, then show a message you write.

    Show it working
    Point to the three actions in order. Swap two, predict the result, and try it.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep the block stack or a drawing of it. Circle the pair you swapped.

Interactive fiction

Midnight Newsroom

A strange sound has interrupted the last radio show. Write an interactive mystery in which the listener decides which lead to follow.

What you’ll make An original branching mystery with two playable endings and a clue that changes one outcome.

Adjust the challenge

Start small. Tell your first scene aloud. A partner can type your two choices exactly as you say them.

Go further. Give the same clue two plausible meanings. Watch what a reader infers before you reveal the ending.

  1. 1
    Design

    Interrupt the broadcast

    Invent the sound and two people who might explain it. Write a first scene that gives the listener a reason to call either person.

    Show it working
    Ask a reader to predict where each call leads before they choose one.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep the opening line, the two choices, and one prediction your reader made.
    Write the openingRead unit 4Add skill evidence
  2. 2
    State and variables

    Let the radio remember

    In World Builder, name your resource Signals. Start at zero. Let one call earn a signal and make a secret reply require one.

    Show it working
    Reach the same conversation with zero signals and with one. Show when the secret reply becomes available.
    Keep as evidence
    Record the two signal counts and the choice available in each run.
    Open World BuilderRead unit 5Add skill evidence
  3. 3
    Remixing and credit

    Borrow a device, change its purpose

    Play Lantern Routes. Borrow its idea of spending light to send a signal. In your story, invent a different cost and rewrite what the listener hears.

    Show it working
    Show a partner the original decision and your version. Ask them how the cost changes the choice.
    Keep as evidence
    Write a credit naming the starter, the idea you borrowed, and the line or rule you wrote.
    Explore Lantern RoutesRead unit 8Add skill evidence
  4. 4
    Debugging

    Premiere both endings

    Trace one route to each ending. If a required reply stays locked, change the signal reward or the requirement and retry that route.

    Show it working
    Start fresh for each playthrough. Let a reader reach both endings without your help.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep the two choice sequences and the first place you checked for an unreachable ending.

Simulation

Rooftop Water Lab

Your roof has two gardens and one small rain tank. Model how far the water goes, then challenge a friend to find a better route.

What you’ll make A water-delivery simulation with a hand-drawn prediction and a two-run Python experiment.

Adjust the challenge

Start small. Use three counters as buckets. Move them by hand before building the digital version.

Go further. Keep total water fixed and change one route cost. Explain which result comes from your assumption.

  1. 1
    State and variables

    Price two garden routes

    Start with three buckets. Give one garden route a cost of one and the other a cost of two. Decide what spending a bucket represents.

    Show it working
    Trace both trips with counters. Show how many buckets reach each garden.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep two rows with starting water, route cost, and water left.
    Model both routesRead unit 5Add skill evidence
  2. 2
    Data and models

    Turn a route into data

    Open the Lantern Routes Python file. Rename its two places for your gardens and find the cost stored beside each name.

    Show it working
    Point to a cost in ROUTES. Run its route and connect the number in the file to the amount left on screen.
    Keep as evidence
    Save one edited route entry with the matching result from its run.
    Get the Python starterRead unit 6Add skill evidence
  3. 3
    Debugging

    Test a leaky pipe

    Run one route twice. Keep the starting amount and signal choice fixed. For the second run, raise only that route’s cost by one.

    Show it working
    Predict the new amount before running. If the ending changes, use the threshold in the code to explain why.
    Keep as evidence
    Write the changed input and both observed outputs. Did the amount, the ending, or both change?
    Open the experiment briefRead unit 7Add skill evidence
  4. 4
    Communication

    Defend a garden design

    Invite a visitor to choose a garden. Show your two results and ask what this small tank model leaves out.

    Show it working
    Recommend a route for a named goal. Give a number from your experiment and one limit of the model.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep the recommendation, one result, and a question for your next experiment.

Creative gifts

Playable Postcards

Give someone a tiny world they can open and explore. Hide a joke, a thank-you, or a made-up celebration behind choices you design.

What you’ll make A digital gift with two surprises, original writing, and a downloadable playable copy.

Adjust the challenge

Start small. Draw two doors. Tell a partner what waits behind each one, then use your words as the scene text.

Go further. Let the two routes meet again. Change the final surprise depending on a keepsake the visitor found earlier.

  1. 1
    Design

    Design the first ten seconds

    Invent an occasion and decide how the visitor should feel. Write the invitation and sketch the first thing they can do.

    Show it working
    Read only your opening to a partner. Ask them what they would try first.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep your sketch and one word your visitor used to describe the opening.
    Sketch my giftRead unit 1Add skill evidence
  2. 2
    Rules and decisions

    Make two surprises

    Build a front-door scene with two choices and a different ending behind each. Write the endings yourself.

    Show it working
    Play both paths from the opening. Explain which click reveals which surprise.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep a three-box map with both choices labeled.
    Build the postcardRead unit 4Add skill evidence
  3. 3
    Debugging

    Make the invitation easier to use

    Give the postcard to a first-time player. If they hesitate, rewrite one choice so the action is easier to picture.

    Show it working
    Show the first and revised versions. Ask the player to point to the route they want.
    Keep as evidence
    Save one before-and-after choice label and what the player did when they saw it.
    Revise my choicesRead unit 9Add skill evidence
  4. 4
    Communication

    Deliver a working gift

    Choose Download playable game in World Builder. Open the downloaded file, replay both surprises, then let a guest try it.

    Show it working
    Hand off the game with one line written for your guest. Ask which detail felt made for them.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep the playable file and a note on one detail your guest noticed.

Sports

Backyard Scoreboard

Invent a two-button playground sport. A quick move and a trick move earn different points. Pick the winning score, then test how the rule feels.

What you’ll make A MakeCode Arcade scorekeeper and a written explanation of the first winning turn.

Adjust the challenge

Start small. Start with paper tokens and a target of three. Act out the two button presses before opening the editor.

Go further. Find two sequences that reach the same winning score in different numbers of turns.

  1. 1
    Rules and decisions

    Invent two scoring moves

    Name two moves from a sport you know or invent. Make the A move worth one point and the B move worth two.

    Show it working
    Ask a partner to call A, B, A while you move counters. Explain why the last total is four.
    Keep as evidence
    Save the two scoring rules and the three running totals.
    Plan my scoring rulesRead unit 2Add skill evidence
  2. 2
    State and variables

    Put the score on screen

    Start a new MakeCode Arcade project. In Controller, add A and B pressed events. In Info, put change score by 1 in A and change score by 2 in B.

    Show it working
    Restart the simulator and replay A, B, A. Compare the score after each press with your paper trace.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep a sketch or screenshot of both events with the simulator’s final score.
    Open MakeCode ArcadeRead unit 5Add skill evidence
  3. 3
    Debugging

    Catch a skipped win

    After each scoring block, add an if score = 3 with a game over win block inside. Predict what happens if someone presses B twice.

    Show it working
    Try B, B from zero. Replace equals with greater-than-or-equal and replay. Show what happens at three points and at four.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep the input sequence that exposed the skip and the condition that fixed it.

  4. 4
    Remixing and credit

    Let someone invent a league

    Keep a copy of your first rules. Invite a tester to suggest one scoring change, give them credit, and choose whether to put it in a second version.

    Show it working
    Play the same sequence under both rules. Explain which version you would use for a short round.
    Keep as evidence
    Keep both scores, credit the suggestion, and describe the rule you chose.
